# Attention and KV cache: technical report

In this completed TinyStories experiment, grouped-query attention (GQA) achieved essentially the same held-out prediction quality as multi-head attention (MHA) while using 75% less KV-cache storage. Multi-query attention (MQA) used 87.5% less cache storage with a small measured quality penalty. Caching substantially accelerated decoding; reducing KV heads produced smaller, workload-dependent timing differences. These observations describe one training seed on an Apple M2 Pro using FP32 PyTorch MPS and explicit attention computation. They do not establish a universal architecture ranking.

This report analyzes saved `run_001` evidence. Preparing the documentation did not rerun training, evaluation, generation, benchmarks, or runtime tests. The [verification record](../results/run_001/verification.json) records the artifact checks and comparison with the frozen run source. The [artifact manifest](../results/run_001/artifact_manifest.json) identifies the exported evidence; the [results README](../results/run_001/README.md) describes its scope and any path redactions. A [plain-language report](non-technical-report.md) explains the same findings without assuming attention knowledge.

The experimental question was how sharing key/value heads changes prediction quality, cache storage, and decoding latency when model width, depth, query heads, tokenizer, data, and training-token budget are held constant. Each of six decoder layers has width 384, feed-forward width 1,024, eight query heads, and head dimension 48. MHA has eight KV heads, GQA two, and MQA one. The models use RoPE, RMSNorm, SwiGLU, tied input/output embeddings, and bias-free projections. Reducing KV projections changes the parameter count; this is an equal-token-budget comparison, not an equal-parameter comparison. The complete settings are in the [experiment configuration](../results/run_001/config/experiment.json).

The training set contains 250,000 selected unique normalized TinyStories stories. The unique official validation split was deterministically divided into 10,995 validation and 10,995 test stories; the test partition is therefore derived, not an official test split. Normalization standardizes Unicode and line endings and trims outer whitespace. Exact normalized duplicates and exact overlap with held-out stories were excluded before training selection. This does not detect near duplicates or paraphrases. A shared 8,192-token byte-level BPE tokenizer was fitted on training text only. [Split manifest](../results/run_001/data_manifest/splits.json).

Stories were packed with beginning/end markers into contiguous token streams. Attention can cross story boundaries; an end marker does not reset attention. Training uses 1,024-token windows and discards 719 incomplete tail targets per epoch. Held-out tails are retained with padding targets excluded from the denominator. There are 54,879,232 eligible training targets per epoch, 2,354,442 validation targets, and 2,358,938 test targets. This distinction between stories, stored tokens, and scored targets matters when comparing budgets or aggregating loss. [Token manifest](../results/run_001/data_manifest/tokens.json).

Each variant completed three epochs, 20,100 optimizer updates, and 164,637,696 processed target tokens with seed 2027. Four windows per microbatch and two accumulation steps give 8,192 targets in a full update; the final group is smaller. AdamW uses a maximum learning rate of 0.0003, a minimum of 0.00003, 5% warm-up, betas (0.9, 0.95), weight decay 0.1, and gradient clipping at 1.0. The primary evaluation uses the final checkpoint after the equal fixed budget. Validation-best checkpoints are secondary; all three runs reached their lowest recorded validation loss at the final epoch. [MHA result](../results/run_001/models/mha/result.json), [GQA result](../results/run_001/models/gqa/result.json), [MQA result](../results/run_001/models/mqa/result.json).

Negative log-likelihood (NLL) measures the model's prediction loss; lower is better. Perplexity is the exponential of aggregate token-weighted NLL, rather than an average of window perplexities. The identical test denominator and tokenizer make the following within-experiment comparison meaningful. The unigram reference predicts from smoothed token frequencies without context.

The GQA–MHA gap is only 0.00196 perplexity, or 0.000390 NLL. It supports “nearly equal measured quality,” not “GQA improves quality”: one seed gives no estimate of training-seed variation. MQA's 0.03250 perplexity increase is small in this setting, but its acceptability would depend on the application. Neither metric directly scores story coherence or instruction following.

The overlapping training curves show similar learning trajectories. Full-validation measurements occur at the three epoch boundaries, rather than at every training point. MHA validation NLL fell from 1.84452 to 1.67488 to 1.61047; GQA from 1.84623 to 1.67427 to 1.61043; MQA from 1.85172 to 1.68009 to 1.61694. Continued improvement at the endpoint means the fixed budget does not establish convergence. The large initial loss also makes small late differences hard to see at this plot scale; use the numerical records for those differences. [MHA metrics](../results/run_001/models/mha/metrics.csv), [GQA metrics](../results/run_001/models/gqa/metrics.csv), [MQA metrics](../results/run_001/models/mqa/metrics.csv).

All variants use the same grouped batched-matrix-multiplication attention path. It keeps K/V compact instead of expanding their stored heads, but materializes attention scores. This is not fused FlashAttention or a comparison of the fastest available kernels. A reusable model-local causal mask handles full/chunked forwards, while one-token cached decoding attends to the entire stored prefix without constructing a mask. Cached and uncached benchmark paths both project only the final hidden position into vocabulary logits. These choices reduce avoidable baseline work while preserving a common implementation. [Benchmark metadata](../results/run_001/benchmarks/gqa/metadata.json).

The exact logical cache formula is `2 × layers × batch × KV_heads × active_tokens × head_dimension × bytes_per_value`. The factor two counts keys and values; FP32 contributes four bytes per value. With every other dimension fixed, GQA stores one-quarter and MQA one-eighth of MHA's KV tensors. At batch four after a 512-token prompt and 128 append steps, the totals are 45, 11.25, and 5.625 MiB respectively. These are cache savings, not 75% or 87.5% reductions in total application memory. Model weights, intermediate activations, runtime allocations, and other processes remain relevant.

![Logical and allocated KV storage at the completed benchmark lengths](../results/run_001/figures/cache_growth.png)

The straight lines reflect linear storage growth with retained token count. Logical and allocated lines overlap here because each request reserves exactly prompt length plus 128 positions and fills them by the final measurement. They need not match earlier in a request: the [cache walkthrough](../results/run_001/evaluation/cache_growth_gqa.json) records a fixed allocated capacity while logical occupancy grows. The figure's horizontal axis includes the appended positions; it is not the prompt length alone. [Quality/cost table](../results/run_001/quality_cost.csv).

Benchmarks cover prompt lengths 128, 256, 512, 1,024, and 2,048 at batches one and four: ten conditions per model. Each condition has five warm-ups and twenty measured trials, giving 750 raw paired-mode rows and 600 measured rows across the three models. Each row contains both cached and uncached work. Both modes consume identical fixed held-out token values and execute exactly 128 append forwards; EOS cannot shorten a trial. This is not ordinary sampled generation, where the first new token can come from prefill logits. [Benchmark audit](../results/run_001/benchmark_audit.json).

Synchronization brackets timed regions. Cache allocation, input transfer, tokenization, progress display, logging, and parity checks are excluded; runtime overhead inside model calls remains included. Prefill—the initial full-prompt pass—is timed separately from append decoding. The saved correctness record passed at `atol=1e-5`, `rtol=1e-4`, and all thirty condition-specific parity records passed before valid timing. Parity compares equivalent cached/full execution of a given trained model; it does not require different architectures to produce equal predictions. [Correctness record](../results/run_001/evaluation/correctness.json), [example workload parity](../results/run_001/benchmarks/mha/parity_p512_b4.json).

The most useful in-context illustration is a 512-token prompt, batch four, and 128 append steps, ending at 640 positions. Each latency below is the median across trials of the trial's average duration per append step. A batch-four step processes four tokens, one per sequence.

| Variant | Cached ms/step | Uncached ms/step | Uncached/cached ratio | Cached tokens/s | Cached prefill ms |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| MHA | 7.443 | 61.146 | 8.22× | 537.44 | 53.050 |
| GQA | 6.811 | 56.030 | 8.23× | 587.30 | 47.930 |
| MQA | 6.885 | 54.585 | 7.93× | 580.94 | 46.712 |

Source: [quality/cost table](../results/run_001/quality_cost.csv). Ratios divide the reported medians and exclude prefill. GQA's cached median is 8.49% lower than MHA's here; MQA's is 7.49% lower. The roughly eightfold caching benefit is substantially larger than the change from sharing KV heads. The smaller MQA cache does not yield the lowest cached median in this condition.

![Cached and uncached decoding latency by prompt length and batch size](../results/run_001/figures/decode_latency.png)

The steep dashed lines show the cost of repeatedly recomputing the growing prefix. Cached lines remain much flatter because previous keys and values are reused. The full axis range compresses the small differences among cached models. At batch one and prompt lengths 128–512, MHA is slightly faster: its cached medians are 6.291–6.411 ms versus GQA's 6.419–6.485 and MQA's 6.455–6.499. Sharing K/V therefore does not guarantee lower measured latency. [Complete workload table](../results/run_001/quality_cost.csv).

The 1,024-token prompts plus 128 appended tokens and every 2,048-token prompt exceed the trained 1,024-token context. Their results measure system behavior only. At prompt 2,048, batch four, cached medians are 8.578/7.697/7.494 ms for MHA/GQA/MQA, and cached prefill medians are 529.060/425.663/421.897 ms. These figures cannot establish long-context language quality. The [prefill plot](../results/run_001/figures/prefill_latency.png) also shows that cache reuse does not remove the initial prompt-processing cost.

Trial variability and machine state qualify those longer-context comparisons. Each condition balances ten cached-first and ten uncached-first measured trials. Nevertheless, at prompt 1,024 and batch four, cached medians split by execution order differ by roughly 5% for all three variants. The GQA prompt-1,024/batch-one cached p95 is 7.557 ms against a 6.557 ms median, indicating a noisier upper tail. These p95 values summarize trial-average step times; they are not individual-token p95 latency or production service guarantees. [Benchmark audit](../results/run_001/benchmark_audit.json).

During measured MHA prompt-2,048/batch-four trials, system swap snapshots increased from about 1,780 to a maximum of 3,744 MiB. This is evidence of a changing memory-pressure environment, not proof that swapping caused any particular latency. GQA and MQA later inherited substantial swap occupancy; high occupancy alone does not prove active thrashing. Process RSS, MPS live tensor allocation, and Metal driver allocation are overlapping snapshots on unified memory, not additive totals or exact peaks. Inspect the [MHA](../results/run_001/benchmarks/mha/trials.jsonl), [GQA](../results/run_001/benchmarks/gqa/trials.jsonl), and [MQA](../results/run_001/benchmarks/mqa/trials.jsonl) raw rows before assigning a causal explanation.

Saved generation samples expose failure modes that aggregate perplexity misses. The [MHA bridge story](../results/run_001/generation/mha_0655a80d680d.json) shifts toward a fence; the [GQA box story](../results/run_001/generation/gqa_9b2c948b485a.json) repeats actions; the [MQA box story](../results/run_001/generation/mqa_4355824052c5.json) has a mother suggesting they ask “Mom and Dad.” These are illustrative observations under temperature 0.8, top-p 0.9, and a 128-token cap. An unfinished sentence at that cap is not evidence of natural stopping failure, and twelve samples do not support a quality ranking.

The strongest conclusion is structural cache reduction with near-matching GQA quality in this run, plus measured caching benefits for the recorded workloads. One seed, synthetic simplified-English text, unequal parameter counts, exact-only deduplication, explicit attention kernels, and sequential measurements constrain generalization. Useful follow-up experiments would repeat selected comparisons across seeds, control or randomize benchmark order and machine state, compare optimized kernels separately, and evaluate coherent generation and longer-context quality directly. Those experiments remain future work.

This from-scratch comparison draws on [Attention Is All You Need](https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.03762), [Multi-Query Attention](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.02150), [Grouped-Query Attention](https://aclanthology.org/2023.emnlp-main.298/), and [TinyStories](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.07759). The implementations and training budget are specific to this project; it is not a reproduction of the papers' original setups or GQA checkpoint uptraining.
